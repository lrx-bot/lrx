# Chapter 1: The Year of Forgetting

The first time I noticed the world forgetting things was on a Tuesday morning.

The sign above the bakery in front of the station used to have four characters on it. I passed it every day on the way to school, from elementary school all the way to high school, until I could recite it with my eyes closed. But that morning, the sign only had three. Where the third had been was a patch of clean white, as if no one had ever written anything there.

I stood under the sign and thought for a long time. I couldn't remember what the character had been.

"Tokine, you're going to be late."

Sumi came running up behind me, her blue hair tie swinging in the morning light. She grabbed my bag strap and dragged me toward the ticket gates.

"Sumi, what's that shop called?"

"Hm?" She glanced back. "...That's weird. What was it called? I see it every day."

She tilted her head and thought for two seconds, then laughed. "Whatever. It's the place with the really good bread."

Neither of us knew then that it was the first word to be eaten.

---

After that, it happened more and more.

First signs, then textbooks. On page forty-seven of our English textbook there was a word, and the teacher stopped halfway through reading it. She stared at the line for a long time, then said, "...There's a printing error here. Let's skip it."

But I remembered there had been a word there. I remembered its spelling, I remembered its meaning, but no matter how hard I tried, I couldn't say it out loud. As if someone had snipped it off my tongue.

Strange reports began appearing on the news. In some small town, every road sign turned blank overnight. Some country's national anthem lost a line, and no one could remember what it used to say. Experts called it collective memory impairment, stress, electromagnetic waves. They said all kinds of things.

People gave it a name. The Great Forgetting.

But the name itself was slowly fading too. Three months later, hardly anyone mentioned it. Not because it was over, but because people had started forgetting that they were forgetting.

---

I have a guitar.

My father left it to me. He died the year I turned twelve, and all he left me was this guitar and a notebook full of chords. On the back cover, in hurried handwriting, he'd written a line in English: *Words are the only things that outlive us.*

I didn't really understand it back then. It was only in the year the Great Forgetting began that I slowly realized the line could also run the other way. If words die, we die with them.

I started keeping sticky notes in my guitar case. Every night, I would write down the words I'd noticed disappearing that day and stick them inside the lid. Yellow, pink, blue, one layered over another. Some words, when I looked at them the next day, I couldn't recognize anymore, even in my own handwriting. But I kept them anyway.

Sumi said I was like someone holding funerals for words.

"Not funerals," I said. "Gravestones. At least we'll know they were here."

She looked at me for a long time, then took a sticky note from my hand, wrote a word on it, and stuck it right in the middle.

*tomorrow*

"This one isn't allowed to disappear," she said. "I'll keep an eye on it for you."

---

Sumi is my childhood friend.

We lived in the same apartment building. Her family was on the fifth floor, mine on the third. When we were little, she'd run down the stairs every morning and knock on our door until I came out. When we got older, she stopped knocking and waited downstairs instead. The blue hair tie was a present from me for her tenth birthday. She's worn it ever since, and even though it's faded from washing, she won't replace it.

She wanted to go to university abroad. To study marine biology. She said she wanted to know what was at the very bottom of the sea.

"So I'm taking the TOEFL," she announced the summer of our third year of middle school. "You're taking it too, Tokine."

"Why do I have to?"

"Because your English is better than mine. And—" She paused, as if searching for a better reason, then gave up. "And it's more fun together."

So we took it together.

Every day after school, we memorized vocabulary in the study room of the old library. The old library was the oldest building at school. The wooden floor creaked when you walked on it. There was no air conditioning in summer, and the heating came and went in winter. Almost no one went there. We liked it, because it was quiet, and because from the window you could see a little bit of the sea.

A very little bit. In the gap between two buildings, a single line of blue.

"After the test," Sumi said one day, pointing at that line, "let's go see the sea together. Not from here. Let's actually walk down to the shore."

"We go to the beach every year."

"This time's different." She propped her chin in her hand. "The sea after the test is going to be extra blue."

I said yes. I never imagined what that promise would become.

---

It was an evening in November. When we left the library, it was already dark. The streetlights came on one by one, lighting the fallen leaves on the sidewalk.

Sumi walked ahead of me, reciting vocabulary as she went.

"*Ephemeral*, short-lived. *Ephemeral*, short-lived..."

Then, in the middle of it, she stopped.

"Tokine."

"Yeah?"

"...What word was I just saying?"

I looked at her. She looked at me. The streetlight fell across her face. Her expression was calm. So calm it frightened me.

"*Ephemeral*," I said.

"Oh, right." She laughed. "*Ephemeral*. Short-lived."

She turned and kept walking, kept reciting. But I stood where I was and didn't move for a long time.

Because just then, for one second, I'd seen a tiny, tiny blank in her eyes. Exactly like the blank on the bakery sign.

That night, I put a new sticky note in my guitar case. What I wrote on it wasn't a word that had disappeared.

What I wrote was: *Don't forget Sumi.*

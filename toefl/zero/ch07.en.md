# Chapter 7: The Hourglass

We went down to the last level on the first night of June.

Four of us. Me, Mio-senpai, Alice. And Lexi, held in my arms. It was heavy, much heavier than it looked, like carrying an entire library.

One of the flagstones in the round room's floor was loose. Mio-senpai opened it with a brass key her grandmother had left her. Beneath it was another staircase, narrower and steeper than the one above, its steps without a single worn hollow.

"No one has ever walked here," Lexi said.

"Then why is there writing on the walls?" Alice asked.

The flashlight fell on the wall. Names were carved here too, but differently from above. Above, the names grew fainter the farther down you went. Here, they grew clearer. So clear they looked freshly carved, the stone dust not yet settled.

I stopped in front of one of them.

*Allison Kanamori*

Alice saw it too. She reached out and touched the letters, her fingers trembling.

"This," Lexi said softly, "is where forgotten people go. The wall above holds the names of librarians, who are slowly forgotten. This wall holds the names of those the world has forgotten but someone still remembers. As long as one person remembers, the name stays here, and grows clearer."

A little farther down, I saw a second familiar name.

*Shizuku*

Only a first name, no family name. Beside it was a faint scratch, as if someone had tried to carve something and not finished.

Mio-senpai stood there looking at it for a long time. She didn't remember the name. But she reached out and, with her fingernail, followed the scratch and added one stroke.

A letter. *S.*

Farther down was a third.

*Sumi Nanami*

My legs gave a little and I caught myself on the wall. The characters were impossibly clear, every stroke deep, so deep I wondered who had cut them with a knife, stroke by stroke.

Then I understood. It was me. Every night I wrote her in my notebook, wrote her on sticky notes, called her in my dreams. My memory had carved her name here, stroke by stroke.

---

At the bottom of the stairs was a very small room.

So small it could only hold a single bookshelf.

A wooden bookshelf, exactly like the ones in our school library, old and a little crooked. On the frame of the top shelf, in a very ancient script, a number was carved.

*0*

There were books on the shelf. Not many, a few dozen. Each spine bore a name. I looked along them one by one: Japanese, English, scripts I didn't know.

"Who are these?" Alice asked.

"People who remember for others," Lexi said. "Everyone who remembers someone the world has forgotten has a book here. What's written inside is the person they remember."

I saw a very new book. A white cover, a name written on the spine in pencil, like a child's handwriting.

*Alice Kanamori*

Alice pulled it out and opened it. The first page was a drawing: a blonde girl holding the hand of a taller girl. Beneath it was written: *My sister, Allison. She liked black tea.*

She hugged the book to her chest and didn't cry. She just held it, for a long time.

Beside that book was another. A dark grey cover, the spine reading: *Mio Tsurumi*. Mio-senpai didn't open it. She only touched the spine with her finger, then drew her hand back as if the book were hot.

The last one was at the far right of the shelf.

A deep blue cover, the same color as Sumi's hair tie. On the spine was my name.

*Tokine Shirase*

I opened it.

It was all Sumi.

The way she waited downstairs for me. The way she laid her hand on Lexi's cover. Her off-key singing, her pen-chewing, her wrinkled nose. Her chin in her hand saying "the sea after the test is going to be extra blue." Page after page, everything I'd written in my notebook, but clearer and more complete than I'd written it. Some of it I'd already forgotten.

Page twenty-three.

*Sumi's favorite color is coral. In third-grade art class, she said the most beautiful thing at the bottom of the sea is coral, so coral is her favorite color. But she wore the blue hair tie for six years, because Tokine gave it to her.*

I knelt on the floor, hugging the book, and cried like a little child.

---

The hourglass sat on the top shelf. Beneath the "0."

It was small, smaller than my palm. A brass frame, a glass bulb. The sand inside wasn't sand, but something very, very fine that glowed, golden, like melted honey, like the sun in the last second of sunset.

The same color as Lexi's eye.

A line was carved into the base. Not Japanese, not English. Lexi read it to us.

"*One hourglass cannot hold a world. But three can.*"

"What does that mean?" Mio-senpai asked.

Lexi was quiet for a moment.

"This hourglass was left by the first librarian," it said. "The story goes that she used it to hold every word in this world, then handed them, grain by grain, to those who came after. But now there are too many words, and Forgetting is too hungry. One hourglass can't hold them."

"So you need three?"

"Not three hourglasses," Lexi said. "Split the world into three."

I looked up.

"Whoever turns this hourglass," Lexi said, "can split one world into three. Each world would only need to remember part of everything. Forgetting would be split in three as well, and be less hungry. If the people of the three worlds each guard their own share, they might survive."

"What about the forgotten people?" Alice asked. "What about Allison?"

"If there's a world devoted to remembering forgotten things," Lexi said, "there might be a place for her in it."

"And Sumi?"

The golden eye turned to me.

"If there's a world," it said, "devoted to remembering one person."

I gripped the hourglass. It was cold, and burning.

"What's the price?" I asked.

Lexi didn't answer right away.

"Whoever turns it," it said, "becomes the sand. Falling endlessly between the three worlds. In each world there would be only part of her. In none of them would she be whole."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning all three worlds would forget her a little at once. In one world she'd forget words. In one she'd have no name. In one she'd be just someone nobody especially remembers."

The little room was very quiet. I could hear the golden light in the hourglass falling, grain by grain.

"And," Lexi said, "for the three worlds to meet again, someone would have to remember every word across all three worlds. Ten thousand. Not one more, not one less."

"Does someone like that exist?"

"I don't know," Lexi said. "It might take a very, very long time. It might never happen."

I looked down at the hourglass in my hand, then at the deep blue book in my arms.

Page twenty-three. Coral.

"Let me think," I said.

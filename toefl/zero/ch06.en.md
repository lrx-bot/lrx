# Chapter 6: The Sea, Alone

The next day, I went to the sea by myself.

The train took forty minutes. There were few people in the car: an old woman holding a basket of mandarins, a boy in a tracksuit asleep with earphones in. The scenery outside changed station by station, from high-rises to houses, from houses to fields, and finally, in the gap between two mountains, a line of blue appeared.

Exactly like the one outside the old library window. Only much wider.

I got off at a tiny unstaffed station. The platform had a single bench, its wood bleached white by the sea wind. I sat on the bench for a long time, then walked down to the beach.

The April sea wind was still cold. Waves rolled in layer after layer and drew back. The sky was very blue, and so was the sea.

But not as blue as she'd said.

"Sumi," I said to the sea. "You lied."

The sea didn't answer.

---

I sat on the beach until the sun went down.

I thought about so many things. The way she waited downstairs every morning. The way she first laid her hand on Lexi's cover in the basement. Her off-key singing, the way she chewed her pen without noticing while memorizing words, the way her nose wrinkled when she got angry. The way she propped her chin in her hand when she said "the sea after the test is going to be extra blue," that small blue line reflected in her eyes.

I wrote all of it down in my notebook. One page, two pages, ten, twenty. I was afraid I'd forget. Afraid I'd wake up one day and the part of my mind that held her would be blank too.

On page twenty-three, I stopped.

I couldn't remember her favorite color.

Not blue. Blue was the color of the hair tie, which I'd given her. What was her own favorite color? She'd told me. Third grade, art class, she said... she said...

I sat on the beach hugging my notebook and thought for a whole hour.

I couldn't remember.

That night when I got home, I found three sticky notes missing from my guitar case.

---

"You're being eaten," Lexi said.

I was sitting beside the lectern in the basement. Mio-senpai, Shizuku-senpai, and Alice were there. None of them remembered Sumi. I'd told them, told them many times, shown them photos. In the photos there was only me, standing beside a patch of blank, smiling at the blank.

"Tokine," Mio-senpai said very carefully, "are you maybe too tired?"

Only Alice said nothing. She looked at me for a long time, then said quietly, "...I believe you. Because it's the same for me. The whole world says I never had a sister. I'm the only one who remembers."

"Remembering someone the world has forgotten," Lexi said, "means fighting Forgetting for something. It won't let you go. The more you remember, the more it takes from you in exchange. Words, memories, and in the end, yourself."

"Then what should I do?"

"Forget her."

I looked up at it. The golden eye was calm.

"It's the simplest way," Lexi said. "Forget her, and you're safe. Forgetting will lose interest in you."

"No."

"You'll die. No, not die. Cease to exist. Just like her."

"Then just like her."

Lexi looked at me for a long time. Then it closed its eye.

"...You're very much like her," it said. "That girl who said, 'We'll remember you.'"

---

April passed. May came.

Forgetting grew faster. Whole districts began to disappear. A news anchor forgot her script during a live broadcast and stared silently into the camera for a full minute before the broadcast cut out. On supermarket shelves, labels turned blank row by row. People began to stop in the middle of the street and stand there, unable to remember where they'd been going.

In mid-May, Shizuku-senpai was gone.

That afternoon, Mio-senpai came down to the basement alone. She was holding a bouquet of white flowers, standing in front of the lectern with a puzzled expression.

"I don't know why I brought these," she said. "The flowers in the greenhouse bloomed, so I cut some. But... whose greenhouse is that?"

She stood there, trying very hard to think. So hard her brows knit together.

"There was someone," she said. "Someone who could finish my sentences. Her name... started with S..."

She crouched down and, with her fingernail, scratched a letter into the stone of the last step.

*S*

Then she looked at the letter and cried. She didn't know why she was crying.

I stood beside her and couldn't do anything. I remembered Shizuku-senpai. I remembered her headphones, her eyes curving into two lines, her saying "every flower has a name." But when I said it aloud, Mio-senpai only looked at me and shook her head.

"I'm sorry, Tokine," she said. "I can't remember. But it hurts here."

She pressed a hand to her chest.

---

That night, Lexi asked me to stay behind.

"At this rate," it said, "by summer this world won't exist."

I didn't say anything. I'd already guessed.

"Every person, every word, every street and sea. All of it will become white. No one will remember there was ever a world here. Not even me. Because when the last person in the world forgets words, a dictionary is just a stack of blank paper."

"Is there a way?" I asked. The same question as many months before.

This time, Lexi wasn't silent.

"There's one," it said. "But you won't like it."

"Tell me."

The golden eye looked at me. In that eye, for the first time, I saw fear.

"I've never been down there," it said. "But every word written there is in my pages. At the very bottom of the stairs, on Shelf Zero, there is an hourglass."

# Chapter 12: The Fourth One

Ten thousand.

When the last grain of sand lit up, I was standing in front of Shelf Zero.

I hadn't stood in a long time. I looked down at my hands. Fingers, lines on the palms, warmth. The hourglass clip was still in my hair, and all the sand inside had fallen. Not a single grain remained.

The books on the shelf had grown from a few dozen to ten thousand. Every one of them glowed. I pulled one out. No name on the spine. I opened it, and inside was a single word.

*ephemeral*

Short-lived. The word Sumi forgot halfway through under the streetlight.

I pulled out another. *lodestar*. Another. *irreversible*. Another. *tomorrow*.

Ten thousand books, ten thousand words. All remembered by that one person.

---

Footsteps sounded on the stairs.

Three staircases, from three directions, leading into the same room. The amber one, the ink-black one, the ordinary one.

The first to come down was a girl with a blue hair tie. She was clutching a test sheet covered in words, her eyes red, as if she'd cried the whole way.

The second was a librarian with a silver-grey braid. She was holding Lexi, her face a little pale, squinting when she looked at things. I knew there was no color left in her world.

The third was a boy. In his pocket was a sheet of paper worn soft from being unfolded. I could see a line peeking out at the corner: a girl's signature.

They looked at one another, and none of them knew the others. But all of them felt they'd seen each other somewhere.

Of course they had. They were three children split from the same world.

"Welcome to Shelf Zero," I said. "...I've waited a long time for you."

---

What happened next, you may already have seen.

I told them how the world was divided. Told them that in every world, I was only part of myself. Told Lexi that the hourglass that split the world was filled with its own ink.

On the bottom shelf, Shiori found Lexi's diary from before it became a dictionary. On the last page was a signature.

Kotoha.

A leaf that keeps words.

Hundreds of millions of years ago, the first girl to walk down these stairs. She wished to remember every word in the world, and so she became a dictionary and stayed alone in the dark for a long, long time, long enough to forget her own name.

"Your name is Kotoha," Shiori said. "I'll remember it for you."

Lexi cried. Not tears, but ink. Drop by drop it fell on the pages, becoming one new word after another. Then it said, very awkwardly, that Lexi was fine, Kotoha was too embarrassing.

I laughed. I hadn't laughed in a long, long time.

I thought of long ago, in the round room, when Sumi put her hand on its cover and said, "We'll remember you."

Look, Sumi. Someone remembers it now.

---

Then Sumi asked me:

"Tokine. Which world are you going back to?"

I looked at her. There was no blank in her eyes. None at all.

In the original world, I lost her. In the amber world, we met again and again, lost each other again and again, remembered each other again and again. She paid half the price for me. She taught me that even if you forget ten thousand times, you can remember again on the ten thousand and first.

"...In every world," I said, "I was only part of myself. Starting today, I can finally be all of me."

"So the place I'm going back to is where someone is waiting for me."

I took off the hourglass clip.

All the sand inside had fallen. It didn't need to be turned anymore. I set it on Shelf Zero, beneath the "0." In the same place it had been long, long ago, the first time I saw it.

Then I took Sumi's hand.

Her hand was warm.

---

I let Shiori and the boy go back first. Someone was waiting for them in their worlds too.

In Shiori's world, there was a blonde librarian who'd write her memoir in awful handwriting. A black-haired senpai whose greenhouse flowers bloomed and fell and bloomed again. A girl named Chinatsu who ate lunch with her every day. She couldn't see color and couldn't hear her own name. But she remembered everyone. And now, someone remembered her too.

In the boy's world, there was someone who'd signed a treaty with him, or someone he'd written English letters to, or someone waiting for him in the study room after closing, or someone who'd lent him an umbrella on a rainy day at a convenience store.

They climbed the stairs, and halfway up, they both turned to look back.

On Shelf Zero, ten thousand books were glowing.

---

Last, there was one more person.

I turned around.

Not toward Sumi, not toward Shiori, not toward the boy.

I was looking at you.

Yes, you. You, reading this book.

I don't know your name. I can't see your face. I only know that outside the three worlds, someone remembered ten thousand words, one by one. Sometimes fast, sometimes nothing for days. Getting many wrong, and starting over.

Do you know what you did?

In the original world, Forgetting ate every word, every person, every street and sea. I split the world into three because one world couldn't hold that much remembering. I thought it would take a very, very long time, maybe forever, for someone to come who could connect them again.

Then you came.

Maybe you only wanted a better score. Maybe you wanted to study somewhere far away. Maybe you just felt that if you remembered more words, the world would get a little bigger.

Whatever the reason, it doesn't matter.

Every word you remembered is a thread. A thread connecting people to the world. You remembered ten thousand.

*Words are the only things that outlive us.*

My father wrote that on the back cover of his guitar notebook. Long ago, I thought it could also be read the other way: if words die, we die with them.

Now I know it can also be read like this:

As long as one person remembers the words, we won't disappear.

Thank you, fourth one.

The last book on Shelf Zero was written for you. It's this one, the one in your hands.

---

It was Monday.

A real Monday. Not the one in the loop. The first day of a new week, after the test.

At seven in the morning, someone knocked on our door. Kept knocking, and knocking, until I came out.

I opened the door.

Sumi stood there, out of breath, her blue hair tie crooked. She was holding two rice balls.

"Tuna mayo and plum. You pick."

"Plum."

"Knew it."

We rode the train for forty minutes. There were few people in the car. The scenery changed station by station, from high-rises to houses, from houses to fields, and finally, in the gap between two mountains, a line of blue appeared.

We got off at the tiny unstaffed station. The bench on the platform, its wood bleached white by the sea wind.

We walked down to the beach.

The sea was blue.

Bluer than in any loop. Far bluer than the sea I saw alone in the original world.

"Tokine," Sumi said. "See? I told you. The sea after the test is extra blue."

"Yeah."

She bent down and picked a shell off the sand. Coral-colored. She placed it in my palm.

"For you," she said. "This time you put it on your windowsill."

I held the shell and looked at her. The wind tangled her hair; she laughed and tried to hold it down, and couldn't.

"Sumi."

"Hm?"

"Tomorrow," I said, "let's come again."

She paused, then smiled. Not a sleepy smile, not an apologetic one. The most brilliant smile I've ever seen in my life.

"Tomorrow," she said. "That word isn't allowed to disappear."

"Yeah," I said. "I'll keep an eye on it for you."

The waves rolled in and drew back. Layer, layer, layer. Like sand falling in an hourglass. But this time, no one would ever need to turn it over again.

Time moved forward.

So did we.

*Zerogatari · End*

*For you, who remembered ten thousand words.*

# Chapter 2: The Dictionary Below

It was Sumi who found the basement of the old library.

One afternoon in December, the heating in the study room broke again. Sumi couldn't stand the cold and said she'd go look for a spare space heater. Behind the last bookshelf on the first floor, she found a very low wooden door. It had no lock, only a brass plate whose lettering had been worn illegible.

"Tokine, come look."

Behind the door was a staircase going down. Stone steps, each one worn into a hollow in the middle, as if many, many people had walked them. The staircase was narrow, wide enough for only one person. Looking down, there was nothing but darkness without a bottom.

"...Our school has a basement?"

"Never heard of one."

Sumi turned on her phone's flashlight and looked back at me, her eyes bright. I knew that look. It was the same look she'd had when we were kids and she wanted to climb onto the apartment roof, or go hunting for fireflies on the hill out back.

"Just one look," she said.

We went down.

---

The steps were longer than I'd imagined. When I reached the hundred and twentieth, I gave up counting. Writing began to appear on the walls. Not graffiti. Names, carved into the stone. Some in Japanese, some in English, some in scripts I didn't recognize. Some had years carved beside them: 1987, 1952, 1909. The farther down we went, the earlier the years.

"Who are these people?" Sumi whispered.

I didn't know. But I noticed something: the farther down we went, the fainter the names became. Not faint as if worn away, but faint like a photograph left in the sun for years, the color fading little by little.

As if they'd been forgotten.

At the bottom of the stairs was a round room. It was so large that the flashlight couldn't reach the walls. Thick dust covered the floor, and in the middle stood a wooden lectern. On the lectern lay an open book.

A very large book. Three times the size of our English dictionaries. The cover was deep red leather, the corners capped in blackened metal.

Sumi walked over and reached out to touch it.

"Don't touch."

The voice wasn't mine.

---

On the cover of the book, an eye opened.

Golden. Like melted honey, or the sun in the last second of sunset. The eye blinked once, then turned, looking first at Sumi, then at me.

Sumi screamed and stumbled back into me. I grabbed her hand. It was shaking. So was mine.

"No need to be afraid," the book said. Its voice was strange. Neither a man's nor a woman's, neither old nor young. Like many voices layered over one another and pressed into one. "I haven't seen a living person in a long time. The last time someone came down here was... hm. I've forgotten."

"What... are you?" I asked.

"A dictionary," it said. "You can call me Lexi. It's not my name, but I don't remember my name anymore, so this will do."

"Dictionaries can talk?"

"Usually not." The eye narrowed, as if smiling. "But I'm not an ordinary dictionary. I remember every word in this world. Every word ever spoken, ever written, ever thought of. All of them are in my pages."

Sumi peeked out from behind me. "Every word?"

"Every one."

"Then..." Sumi hesitated. "The name of the bakery in front of the station. Do you know it?"

The golden eye looked at her for a long time.

"I do," it said. "But I can't tell you."

"Why not?"

"Because that word has already been eaten. I remember it, but this world doesn't anymore. A word that exists only in a dictionary can't be spoken. The moment it's said aloud, it dies a second time."

---

Lexi told us the truth about the Great Forgetting.

It said that in this world there is a thing with no shape and no sound, like fog, like the tide. It's called Forgetting. Not the kind of forgetting we usually mean, not forgetting your keys or a classmate's birthday. It's a kind of hunger.

"It eats words," Lexi said. "Once a word is eaten, everything connected to it grows faint. The letters on signs, the vocabulary in textbooks, the memories in people's heads. And then it goes after the people connected to those things."

"People?"

"Words are the threads between people and the world." The golden eye turned to me. "The names a person has been called, the things they've said, the way others remember them. All of that is words. When every word attached to a person has been eaten, that person stops existing. Not dead. Nonexistent. As if they were never born."

I thought of that one-second blank in Sumi's eyes.

"Once," Lexi said, "there were people in this world whose job was to fight it. They were called librarians. Librarians could see the words that Forgetting had eaten, and could seal them into pages so they wouldn't vanish completely. The names on the stairwell walls are the librarians of past generations."

"And now?"

"Now there are none." Its voice was very calm. "The last librarian used up her ink thirty years ago. Since then, no one has come down these stairs. Until today."

---

We stayed in that round room until dark.

Lexi told us a lot. Why Forgetting kept getting hungrier: because people spoke less, read less, were willing to remember less. What a librarian's ink was: their own memories; every word sealed used up a little of themselves. That there was one more level at the very bottom of the stairs, and even it had never been there.

At last, Sumi asked a question.

"Lexi, how long have you been here by yourself?"

The golden eye blinked.

"...I don't remember. A long time. Long enough that I've forgotten what I originally was."

"Isn't it lonely?"

The eye looked at her for a long, long time. So long I thought it wouldn't answer.

"A dictionary that remembers every word," it said finally, "and not one person who remembers it. What do you think?"

Sumi was quiet for a moment. Then she walked up to the lectern, reached out, and laid her hand gently on the deep red cover. This time, Lexi didn't say "Don't touch."

"Then we'll come often," Sumi said. "Tokine and me. We'll remember you."

Lexi didn't say anything. But I saw its golden eye, in the flashlight beam, go wet for just a moment.

Maybe it was only dust. Maybe not.

---

On the way home, Sumi was happy the whole time, talking about bringing snacks down next time, about reading Lexi her favorite poems.

I didn't say anything.

I was thinking about what Lexi had said. *When every word attached to a person has been eaten, that person stops existing.*

I was thinking about what I would do if, one day, the words attached to Sumi were eaten.

That night I had a dream. In the dream I was on that staircase, going down, down and down, the names on the walls growing fainter. When I reached the very bottom, I saw a new name carved into the wall.

It was mine.

When I woke, it was still dark outside. I opened my guitar case and looked at the sticky note that said *tomorrow*. I looked at it for a long, long time, until dawn.

# Chapter 8: Three Kinds of Remembering

I thought for three days.

On the first day, I went to the station. Not to the sea. I just sat on a bench on the platform and watched people come and go.

Forgetting had already reached here. Half the characters on the station sign were blank. The timetable on the electronic board flickered, sometimes showing, sometimes just a patch of grey. People stood on the platform, and many of them had the same expression: dazed, as if they'd just woken from a very long dream and didn't yet know where they were.

A boy sat down beside me.

A middle schooler in a slightly oversized uniform, hugging an English vocabulary book. The book was old, its corners curled, every page covered in writing. He stared at the timetable across the platform for a long time, then turned to me and asked, a little embarrassed:

"Um... excuse me. Where does this train go?"

"Where are you going?"

He opened his mouth, then stopped.

"...I don't know," he said. "I knew a second ago. I was going somewhere... somewhere important... but I can't remember."

He looked down at the vocabulary book in his hands and opened to the first page. On it, in handwriting pressed hard into the paper, was a line in English:

*I will study abroad. I will see the world.*

"Did you write this?" I asked.

"...I think so." He looked at the line as if someone else had written it. "I want to study overseas. But I can't remember why."

I looked at him. In his eyes was a blank like Sumi's. Small. Still very small.

"You want to see the world," I said, "because the place you live is too small. You want to know what's on the other side of the mountains, the other side of the sea. You want to meet people who speak different languages, and talk to them in theirs."

He stared at me blankly. "...How do you know?"

"I don't," I said. "I just think anyone who writes that on the first page probably feels that way."

He looked at the line again. Then, slowly, the blank in his eyes shrank a little.

"...Thank you." He stood up. "I remember now. I'm going to cram school."

He ran onto a train. Before the doors closed, he turned and waved at me.

I didn't know his name. It was a long, long time before I found out.

His name was Yuu Aihara.

---

On the second day, I went to the greenhouse.

Shizuku-senpai's greenhouse. No one remembered whose it was anymore. But the flowers were still blooming, white flowers, blooming in abundance. Mio-senpai came every day to water them. She didn't know why. She just felt she should.

"Tokine." She saw me and set down the watering can. "Have you decided?"

"Not yet."

We sat side by side on the greenhouse steps. Sunlight came through the glass, warm.

"Senpai, if the world were split in three, which part would you want?"

Mio-senpai thought for a long time.

"I'd want a world," she said, "that can remember forgotten things."

"For S?"

She smiled a little, bitterly. "I don't even remember who she was. But I know there was someone I was never supposed to forget. If there were a world where forgotten things don't vanish completely, where someone goes looking for them and writes them down... then maybe in that world, I could find her again."

"That world would hurt," I said. "Lexi says remembering forgotten things has a price."

"I know," Mio-senpai said. "But remembering that doesn't hurt probably isn't really remembering."

Alice said the same. That night in the basement, hugging the white book, she told me:

"Give me a world where I can get Allison back. Even if I have to pay for it. Even if that world is dark."

"Alice—"

"Tokine, don't you get it?" She looked up, her blue eyes red. "The whole world forgot her. I'm the only one who remembers. If even I give up, she's really gone."

I got it. I got it better than anyone.

---

On the third day, I went back to Shelf Zero.

I put the deep blue book back in its place. Then I sat in front of the shelf and set Lexi on my knees.

"Lexi."

"Mm."

"If the world were split in three, which part would you want?"

The golden eye blinked, as if it hadn't expected me to ask.

"...I'm a dictionary," it said. "Dictionaries don't need to want anything."

"But you have wanted," I said. "When Sumi asked if you were lonely, you cried."

Lexi was silent for a long time.

"I want," it said at last, its voice soft, as soft as the sound of a page turning, "someone to remember my name."

"What's your name?"

"I forgot," it said. "I remember every word in the world. This one is the only one I've forgotten."

I laid my hand on its deep red cover, the way Sumi had that day.

"Then there'll be a world," I said, "for finding your name."

---

That night, I wrote the three kinds of remembering in my notebook.

*The first world: remember one person.*

For Sumi. In that world, one person will be remembered by another with everything they have. It might be beautiful. It might be cruel. Lexi said a wish to remember someone too much might make time stop, make the same week repeat over and over.

That's all right. As long as she's there.

*The second world: remember what was forgotten.*

For Allison, for S, for the names fading on the stairwell walls, for Lexi. That world will have librarians, monsters made from forgotten things, prices. It will be dark. People will use themselves up to remember others.

But in that world, forgotten things won't vanish completely.

*The third world: remember where you want to go.*

For the boy on the platform. For everyone standing in the street who has forgotten where they were going. That world will be ordinary. No magic, no loops, no talking dictionaries. Only tests, only studying, only someone memorizing words one by one, then going where they want to go.

That might be the hardest world of all. Because there, no one will remember for you. You can only remember for yourself.

I closed the notebook.

Outside, the June moon was full. In the distance another stretch of street went dark, like a section of blackboard being wiped.

I picked up the hourglass and held it to the moonlight. The golden light inside was flowing, one grain, one grain, one grain.

"Sumi," I said. "Wait for me a little longer."

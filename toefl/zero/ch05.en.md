# Chapter 5: Building B, Seats 11 and 12

The test was on the first Saturday of April.

At seven in the morning, Sumi was waiting for me downstairs. Like every day. She was in her school uniform, her blue hair tie tied tighter than usual, holding two rice balls.

"Tuna mayo and plum. You pick."

"Plum."

"Knew it." She shoved the rice ball at me, then gave me a look. "Tokine, you didn't sleep last night, did you?"

"I did."

"Liar. Your dark circles are about to fall off your chin."

I didn't tell her I'd spent the night looking at photos of her. I had hundreds on my phone, from elementary school until now. I went through them one by one, making sure her face was still clear in each, that it hadn't faded.

Lately, she'd been fading more and more often.

Sometimes it was a word, like *ephemeral* that day. Sometimes it was a memory; she'd suddenly be unable to recall where we'd gone the week before. Once, in the study room, she called my name and stopped halfway, looking at me, a huge blank in her eyes. The blank lasted three seconds. After three seconds she smiled and said "Tokine," as if nothing had happened.

But I knew what had happened. So did Lexi.

"Forgetting is looking for her," Lexi had told me. "She has too many words attached to her. She loves too many things, is willing to remember too many things, is loved by too many people. To Forgetting, someone like that smells the sweetest."

"Is there anything we can do?"

Lexi was silent a long time.

"A librarian can seal a person's words into a page," it said. "But you're not a librarian. And a person sealed into a page no longer exists in this world. It's no different from being eaten."

I didn't ask again.

---

The test center was at a university downtown. Building B, third floor. My seat was number 11; Sumi's was 12. A narrow aisle separated us. She was on my right.

Before we sat down, she pressed something into my hand.

A sticky note. It said: *After the test, we go see the sea. Don't forget.*

"This is my good-luck charm," she said. "I'm lending it to you. Give it back after."

I folded it and put it in my breast pocket.

The test began.

Reading. Listening. My answers came easily. The words I'd memorized over those months rose up one by one, as if someone were whispering them in my ear. Now and then I'd steal a glance to my right. Sumi had her head down, her pen scratching across the scratch paper. Her profile was serious, lips pressed together, exactly as she looked memorizing words in the basement.

During the ten-minute break, we met in the hallway. She stretched.

"Listening passage three was so hard. It was about hydrothermal vents."

"You must have loved it."

"I did!" Her eyes lit up. "There's this shrimp in there whose eyes have degenerated, and it grew a light-sensing organ on its back so it can see the glow from the vents. Isn't that amazing—"

She was mid-sentence when she stopped.

"...Tokine, what's that shrimp called? I knew it a second ago."

I looked at her. The sunlight through the hallway windows was very bright, so bright her outline blurred a little.

"It's fine," I said. "We'll look it up after."

"Mm." She smiled. "We'll look it up after."

---

Speaking. Writing.

The second writing prompt was: "Describe a place you want to return to, and explain why."

I wrote about the basement of the old library. About the round walls, the echo of the guitar, a dictionary with a golden eye. I wrote quickly, barely needing to think.

Halfway through, I heard a soft sound from my right.

The sound of a pen hitting the floor.

I turned my head.

Sumi was still sitting there. Her hands hovered above the keyboard, motionless. On her screen, she'd already written three paragraphs. I saw the start of the first: *The place I want to return to is the sea, with my best friend—*

Then I saw that in the name field at the top of her screen, the characters were fading, one by one.

Nana. Mi. Sumi.

As if being slowly rubbed away by an eraser.

"Sumi," I called. My voice was very quiet; the proctor didn't hear.

She slowly turned to look at me. The blank in her eyes had grown so large it could barely hold her anymore.

"...Tokine?" she said. Then she frowned, as if trying hard to remember something very important. "Tokine. You're Tokine. I..."

"Don't talk," I said. "Don't think. Just look at me."

"I can't remember," she said. "I can't remember who I am. But I remember you. It's so strange. I can't remember anything, only you."

Her hands were growing more and more transparent. I could see the words on her screen through her fingers.

*—with my best friend, Tokine. We promised to go together after the test. I think the sea will be very blue. I think—*

"Tokine," she said. "The sea..."

"We'll go," I said. My tears fell on the keyboard. "Right after the test. You said it yourself: the sea after the test is going to be extra blue."

"Mm." She smiled. I would remember that smile for the rest of my life. Not a brilliant smile. A little sleepy, a little apologetic, the same smile she'd had as a kid every morning when she knocked on our door until I came out.

"Tomorrow," she said. "That word isn't allowed to disappear. Keep an eye on it for me."

And then she was gone.

---

The bell rang to end the test.

The proctor walked down the rows collecting scratch paper. When she reached seat 12, she glanced at it and walked past, perfectly naturally.

Seat 12 was empty. The screen was dark. There was no sign anyone had sat in the chair. No pen on the floor.

"Excuse me," I said, standing up. "Where's the person in seat 12?"

The proctor looked at her list, then at me.

"Twelve? There were only eleven people in this room today. Seat 12 has been empty the whole time."

I ran out of the test room. I ran down the stairs, out the university gate, onto the train, back to our apartment building. I climbed to the fifth floor and rang her doorbell.

Her mother opened the door.

"Auntie, where's Sumi?"

She looked at me, puzzled and gentle, the way you'd look at a lost child.

"Tokine? What's wrong? ...Who's Sumi?"

I stood in the fifth-floor hallway and took the sticky note out of my breast pocket.

*After the test, we go see the sea. Don't forget.*

The words were still there. Her handwriting. Round, a little crooked.

The whole world had forgotten her.

Only this sticky note, and I, still remembered.

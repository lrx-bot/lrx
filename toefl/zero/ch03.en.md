# Chapter 3: The Library Committee

After that, we went down every day after school.

We brought snacks, thermoses, blankets. Sumi read Lexi poems, the ones she loved about the sea. Lexi would correct her pronunciation, then say in a very grudging tone, "...That one's not bad."

The third person to find the staircase was Mio Tsurumi.

That day we were sitting beside the lectern memorizing vocabulary when footsteps sounded on the stairs. Light, steady, one step at a time. Sumi nervously grabbed my sleeve. The footsteps stopped at the last step, and then a flashlight beam swept toward us.

"...So it was you two."

Standing there was a tall girl, black hair falling all the way to her waist, a library committee armband pinned to her left sleeve. She was a year above us. I'd seen her at an all-school assembly, speaking for the library committee. Her voice wasn't loud, but the whole gym had gone quiet to listen.

"Tsurumi-senpai?"

"The borrowing records." She waved a card. "For the past month, two first-years have stayed in the old building until an hour after closing every day, but haven't checked out a single book. I got curious."

Her gaze passed over us and landed on the lectern. Lexi's eye was already closed. It looked like an ordinary, very large old book.

But Mio-senpai stared at it for a long time, and then said something that stunned both Sumi and me.

"...Hello. My name is Mio Tsurumi. My grandmother told me a talking dictionary lived under the old library. I always thought it was a fairy tale."

The golden eye opened.

"Your grandmother," Lexi said. "What was her name?"

"Chizuru Tsurumi."

Lexi was silent a moment. "...I remember her. She was the partner of the last librarian. When the two of them came down these stairs, they were your age."

---

Mio-senpai became our third.

Then there was a fourth.

Shizuku-senpai was Mio-senpai's partner. Vice-chair of the library committee, short hair, always wearing a pair of enormous headphones playing classical music. She spoke slowly, and when she smiled her eyes curved into two lines. She and Mio-senpai had known each other since elementary school. How close were they? Mio-senpai would say the first half of a sentence, and Shizuku-senpai would finish it.

"They're like one person split in half," Sumi whispered to me once.

"So are we, aren't we," I said.

Sumi glanced at me and didn't say anything, but she smiled like she was very happy.

Shizuku-senpai had a little greenhouse behind the old building. A former biology teacher had left it, and after no one looked after it anymore, she took it over and grew flowers. All her flowers were white. She said white flowers were the easiest to forget, so someone had to remember them.

"Every flower has a name," she said as she watered them. "Even if no one calls it, it still does."

---

The fifth was Alice.

Alice Kanamori, same year as us, a half-British girl who'd transferred back from England. Blonde twin-tails, blue eyes, a sharp tongue. The first time she showed up in the basement, she was chasing her sister.

"Allison! You ran off to a place like this again—"

She charged down the last step, then saw the four of us and a book with an open eye, and froze.

"...What is this, a meeting? A cult?"

Her sister, Allison Kanamori, was three years older and studied linguistics at a nearby university. Somewhere, Allison had heard the legend of the old library's basement and had been investigating it in secret. That day she'd gotten there just ahead of Alice and was crouched halfway down the stairs, shining a flashlight on the names on the wall and copying them one by one into a notebook.

"These names are disappearing," Allison said. Her Japanese had a slight accent, but it was gentle. "Last week when I copied them, there were three names here. Today there are only two."

Lexi looked at her for a long time.

"You can see forgotten things," it said. "You have a librarian's eyes."

Alice seized her sister's arm. "What librarian? Allison, let's go. This place is weird."

"Alice." Allison smiled and patted her head. "Haven't you always said you wanted to know why the words in your English textbook are disappearing?"

Alice bit her lip and said nothing.

Later I learned that the first disappearing word Alice ever found was in an English letter her mother had left her. In one sentence of the letter, a word was gone. She'd taken the letter and asked everyone, and no one could tell her what the word had been.

"So," she said to Lexi, still sharp, "if you really remember every word, then remember that one for me."

"I do remember it," Lexi said. "But I can't say it."

"Then what good are you!"

"Some good," Lexi said. "At least it's still somewhere."

Alice glared at it, her eyes red, but not one tear fell.

---

That winter, there were six people in the basement. Seven, if you counted Lexi.

We memorized vocabulary together. Alice criticized everyone's pronunciation, and was hardest on Sumi, who laughed every time and said, "One more time." Mio-senpai turned every subject into stories, told so well that even Lexi would chime in. Shizuku-senpai brought white flowers from the greenhouse and put them in a glass bottle beside the lectern. Allison kept copying the names on the wall, day after day, filling three notebooks.

I played guitar.

The basement had good echo. The sound of the guitar would circle the round walls many times before it faded. Lexi said it hadn't heard music in hundreds of years. Sumi would sing along. She sang a little off-key, but she sang loudly.

It was the best winter of my life.

I still remember every day of that winter. I remember the scent of Shizuku-senpai's white flowers, the metaphor Mio-senpai used to explain photosynthesis, the afternoon Alice first called me "Tokine" instead of "Shirase." I remember Sumi's off-key singing circling the basement over and over until it settled onto my guitar strings.

I remember.

Because later, I was the only one left who did.
